Electrochemical (Voltaic) Cells and Their Applications in Human Life

A scientific exploration of structure, reactions, history, and modern uses.

Voltaic cells convert chemical energy directly into electrical energy through spontaneous redox reactions. From simple zinc–copper cells to advanced lithium-ion batteries and fuel cells, these systems power countless devices and processes in daily life. This website presents their structure, classification, history, and applications using clear chemical language.

By Maja Kurek

Introduction

Electrochemical or voltaic cells are devices that generate electric current from chemical reactions. They consist of two half-cells where oxidation and reduction occur separately. By connecting these compartments through an external conductor and an internal ionic path, electrons and ions flow in controlled directions, producing usable energy. Voltaic cells form the foundation of modern portable electronics, transportation systems, medical devices, and industrial technologies.

Theory of Voltaic Cells

Definition

A voltaic cell is an electrochemical system in which a spontaneous redox reaction generates an electric current. The reaction consists of:

  • Oxidation at the anode
  • Reduction at the cathode

Electron and Ion Flow

  • Electrons travel from anode → cathode through the external wire.
  • Cations and anions move through the electrolyte or salt bridge to maintain charge neutrality.

Half-Cell Representation

A half-cell is written using standard electrochemical notation:

Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s)

Standard Cell Potential

Ecell = E°cathode − E°anode

Salt Bridge

◯

External Circuit

Wire + measuring device (lamp or voltmeter)

A typical voltaic cell contains two separate half-cells connected by a conductive bridge. In the anode compartment, metal atoms lose electrons and form cations. These electrons travel through the wire to the cathode, where the reduction process occurs. The salt bridge allows ions to migrate and prevents charge accumulation that would stop the reaction.

Classification: Reversible vs Non-reversible

🔋 Primary Cells (Non-reversible)

Operate through reactions that cannot be efficiently reversed. Once the reactants are consumed, the cell becomes inactive.

🔄 Secondary Cells (Reversible)

Contain redox pairs capable of being restored by applying an external electric current, making them rechargeable.

Examples of Voltaic Cells

Daniell Cell (Zn–Cu)

Anode: Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻

Cathode: Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)

Anode: Solid zinc is oxidized, releasing zinc ions into the solution and 2 electrons.

Cathode: Copper ions in solution gain 2 electrons and deposit as solid copper metal.

⚡ 1.10 V

Uses: Educational demonstrations; historical development.

Alkaline Zn–MnO₂

Anode: Zn(s) + 2OH⁻ → ZnO(s) + H₂O + 2e⁻

Cathode: 2MnO₂(s) + H₂O + 2e⁻ → Mn₂O₃(s) + 2OH⁻

Anode: Zinc reacts with hydroxide ions to form zinc oxide, water, and releases 2 electrons.

Cathode: Manganese dioxide accepts electrons and combines with water to form manganese(III) oxide and hydroxide ions.

⚡ 1.5 V

Uses: Remote controls, toys, clocks.

Lead–Acid Cell

Anode: Pb(s) + SO₄²⁻ → PbSO₄(s) + 2e⁻

Cathode: PbO₂(s) + SO₄²⁻ + 4H⁺ + 2e⁻ → PbSO₄(s) + 2H₂O

Anode: Lead metal reacts with sulfate ions to form lead sulfate, releasing 2 electrons.

Cathode: Lead dioxide reacts with sulfate ions and hydrogen ions, accepts electrons, forming lead sulfate and water.

⚡ ~2.0 V per cell

Uses: Car starters, backup power.

Lithium-Ion Cell

Anode: C₆Li ⇌ C₆ + Li⁺ + e⁻

Cathode: Li₁₋ₓCoO₂ + xLi⁺ + xe⁻ ⇌ LiCoO₂

Anode: Lithium atoms leave the graphite structure, becoming lithium ions and releasing electrons.

Cathode: Lithium ions insert into the cobalt oxide structure, accepting electrons to balance the charge.

⚡ 3.6–3.7 V

Uses: Smartphones, laptops, EVs.

Nickel–Metal Hydride (NiMH)

Rechargeable nickel-based chemistry with metal hydride anode.

⚡ 1.2 V

Uses: Rechargeable AA/AAA, cameras, tools.

Hydrogen Fuel Cell (PEM)

Anode: H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻

Cathode: O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O

Anode: Hydrogen gas molecules split into hydrogen ions (protons) and electrons.

Cathode: Oxygen gas combines with hydrogen ions and electrons to form water — the only byproduct!

⚡ 0.6–0.7 V per cell

Uses: Clean transportation, backup power.

Interactive Nernst Simulator

Cell potential depends not only on the identity of the electrodes but also on ion concentration and temperature. The Nernst equation describes how these variables influence the voltage. As concentration of reactants increases, the cell potential generally increases. Higher temperature affects the reaction quotient and changes the observed voltage.

Nernst Equation:
E = E° − (0.059/n) × log Q
Calculated Cell Potential 1.10 V

Q = [Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺] = 1.00

Temperature factor (RT/nF): 0.0591 V

When Q = 1, log(Q) = 0, so E = E°

Experimental Protocol

Simple Copper–Zinc Voltaic Cell Experiment

🧪 Materials

  • Zinc strip
  • Copper strip
  • Two beakers
  • 0.1–1.0 M CuSO₄ solution
  • 0.1–1.0 M ZnSO₄ solution
  • Salt bridge (filter paper soaked in KNO₃)
  • Voltmeter
  • Wires + crocodile clips

📋 Procedure

  1. Pour ZnSO₄ into the first beaker and CuSO₄ into the second.
  2. Insert the zinc electrode into the zinc solution and the copper electrode into the copper solution.
  3. Connect the electrodes using wires to a voltmeter.
  4. Place the salt bridge so that it connects both beakers.
  5. Observe the voltage (typically 0.8–1.1 V).
  6. Record any changes over time.

⚠️ Safety Notes

  • Wear goggles and gloves.
  • Do not spill metal salt solutions.
  • Dispose of solutions according to school instructions.
